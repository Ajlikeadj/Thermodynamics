THE RULES BEHIND IT

Four laws
of heat.

Every air conditioner obeys the same four laws of thermodynamics. Scroll to meet them.
0

Zeroth law: temperature is real

If A is in thermal balance with B, and B with C, then A and C are in balance too. That is why a thermometer can measure anything.

Everyday example: A thermometer under your tongue settles at body temperature, so its reading tells you your temperature.

1

First law: energy is never lost

Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only moved or converted. Heat in + work in = heat out.

Everyday example: A pressure cooker: the energy from the stove ends up as heat in the food and steam, none vanishes.

Room heatElectricityHeat outside
2

Second law: heat flows downhill

Heat flows by itself only from hot to cold. Moving it the other way takes work, and some energy is always wasted.

Everyday example: A hot coffee cools to room temperature. It never heats itself back up.

3

Third law: absolute zero is unreachable

As a system nears absolute zero (−273.15 °C), removing the last bit of heat gets harder and harder. You can approach it but never reach it.

Everyday example: Each step of cooling a freezer-cold drink further costs more energy than the last.

How does an air conditioner actually cool?

It doesn’t create cold. It moves heat — on a continuous loop, using pressure, phase changes, and a little bit of clever engineering.

01

Absorb the heat.

Inside your room, the evaporator coil is cold. Warm room air passes over it, and the refrigerant absorbs that heat as it turns from a liquid into a gas.

02

Compress the gas.

The compressor squeezes the low-pressure gas. Squeezing adds energy: the refrigerant becomes a hot, high-pressure gas ready to release its heat outside.

03

Dump the heat.

In the outdoor condenser, heat leaves the refrigerant and escapes into the outside air. The gas cools and condenses back into a liquid.

04

Reset the pressure.

The expansion valve drops the liquid’s pressure and temperature. Now it is cold again — ready to return indoors and repeat the loop.

THE VOCABULARY

System, Surroundings
and Boundary

Before measuring heat, decide what you are studying and where it ends.
Surroundings
Boundary
System

System

The part of the universe you choose to study.

Example: The air and water inside a pressure cooker.

Surroundings

Everything outside the system that can exchange energy or matter with it.

Example: The kitchen and the stove.

Boundary

The real or imaginary surface separating the two. It can be fixed or moving.

Example: The cooker wall, or the stretching skin of a balloon.

Three kinds of system. An open system exchanges matter and energy (a boiling pot with no lid). A closed system exchanges only energy (a sealed can on a stove). An isolated system exchanges neither (an ideal sealed thermos).

Properties of a system

A property is any measurable characteristic that describes the state of a system. Properties come in two families, and one quick test tells them apart: cut the system in half.

Intensive properties

Do not depend on how much matter there is. Cut the system in half and the value stays the same.

  • Temperature: a cup and a bucket of tea can both be 60 °C.
  • Pressure: the air pressure in a small and a big tyre can match.
  • Density: 1 g or 1 kg of water is about 1 g/cm³.

Extensive properties

Do depend on the amount of matter. Cut the system in half and the value halves.

  • Mass: a bucket of water has more than a cup.
  • Volume: a big balloon holds more air than a small one.
  • Total energy: a litre of hot water stores more heat than a spoonful.

Handy trick. Divide an extensive property by mass and you get an intensive one. Volume ÷ mass gives specific volume, and energy ÷ mass gives specific energy.

What is a process?

A process is the path a system follows when it changes from one state to another, such as when its temperature, pressure or volume changes. If a system goes through several processes and ends in its starting state, that sequence is called a cycle. The four-state loop in the T–s diagram above is exactly that.

Isothermal

Temperature constant

Ice melting at 0 °C. Heat goes in, but the temperature holds steady.

Isobaric

Pressure constant

Water boiling in an open pot. The air pressure above it stays the same.

Isochoric

Volume constant

Heating a sealed rigid can. It cannot expand, so the pressure rises.

Adiabatic

No heat exchanged

Pumping a bike tyre quickly. The air warms because there is no time for heat to escape.

THE ENERGY BOOKKEEPING

Energy, Enthalpy,
Entropy and Free Energy

Four state functions that tell you how much energy a change moves, and whether it will happen on its own.

Internal energy (E or U)

The total energy stored inside a system at the molecular level. It does not include the motion or height of the system as a whole.

Kinetic part

Translational + rotational + vibrational motion of the molecules.

Potential part

Intermolecular forces + bond energy (plus electronic and nuclear energy).

First law. ΔE = q + w, where q is heat absorbed by the system and w is work done on the system. At constant pressure, expansion work is w = −PΔV (negative when the system expands).

Enthalpy (H)

Define H = E + PV. At constant pressure, a system goes from state A to B and absorbs heat q. Then EB − EA = q − P(VB − VA), so (EB + PVB) − (EA + PVA) = q. That means ΔH = HB − HA = qP: the enthalpy change equals the heat absorbed at constant pressure. E, P and V are state functions, so H and ΔH are too.

ΔH > 0

Endothermic (Hfinal > Hinitial)

The system absorbs heat, like ice melting.

ΔH < 0

Exothermic (Hinitial > Hfinal)

The system releases heat, like burning fuel.

At constant P

ΔH = ΔE + PΔV

ΔH is larger than ΔE only if the volume increases. If ΔV < 0, ΔH is smaller; if ΔV = 0, they match.

At constant V

ΔE = qV

Heat absorbed at constant volume gives ΔE, not ΔH. In general ΔH = ΔE + VΔP here.

Enthalpy changes in reactions

The enthalpy of formation is the ΔH for forming one mole of a compound from its elements in their standard states. Phase changes have their own enthalpies, and they are not formation reactions.

Formation

  • C(graphite) + 2H2(g) → CH4(g), ΔH = −74.8 kJ/mol
  • H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l), ΔH ≈ −285.8 kJ/mol

Phase changes of water

  • Melting: H2O(s) → H2O(l), ΔH ≈ +6.0 kJ/mol
  • Vaporization: H2O(l) → H2O(g), ΔH ≈ +40.7 kJ/mol at 100 °C (about +44 kJ/mol at 25 °C)

Linking ΔH and ΔE. Volume changes of solids and liquids are tiny, but gases change volume a lot. Treating gases as ideal, PΔV = ΔnRT, so ΔH = ΔE + ΔnRT, where Δn = moles of gaseous products − moles of gaseous reactants. Use it to convert between ΔH and ΔE.

Entropy (S)

Entropy is a state function that tells you whether a change can happen spontaneously: for an isolated system, a spontaneous change has ΔS > 0. Clausius introduced it in 1865. For a reversible Carnot engine between T1 and T2 (T2 > T1), q2 is the heat absorbed at T2 and q1 is the heat rejected at T1. Then (q2 − q1)/q2 = (T2 − T1)/T2, which gives q2/T2 = q1/T1. Counting heat in as positive and heat out as negative, ∑q/T = 0 over a reversible cycle, even though the net heat q is not zero.

Definition. dS = dqrev/T, so at constant temperature ΔS = qrev/T, or qrev = TΔS.

Gibbs free energy (G)

G = H − TS is a state function. At constant temperature, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. At constant T and P, −ΔG is the maximum net useful (non-expansion) work the system can deliver, and a change with ΔG < 0 can occur spontaneously.

Helmholtz work function (A)

  • A = E − TS, so at constant T, ΔA = ΔE − TΔS.
  • For a reversible change, q = TΔS, so ΔA = ΔE − q.
  • With ΔE = q − W (W = work done by the system): ΔA = −Wrev.
  • The decrease in A at constant T is the maximum work obtainable.

Linking G and A

  • At constant P: ΔG = ΔE + PΔV − TΔS.
  • Comparing gives ΔG = ΔA + PΔV = −W + PΔV.
  • So −ΔG = Wmax − PΔV: maximum work minus expansion work against constant pressure.
